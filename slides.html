Part A

Chance and counting

1Why probability?2Sets and sample spaces3Counting4Basic probability rules
The language of chance and the tools to count it.

Many trials reveal the true probability

  • A probability is a number from 0 (can't happen) to 1 (certain), often written as a percentage
  • Three ways to find one: classical (count equally likely outcomes: P(heads) = ½), frequency (the bus was late 6 of 50 days: ≈ 12%), subjective (a reasoned degree of belief)
  • Experiment → outcome → event: rolling a die → a 4 → "an even number"
With a few trials the share can be far from the probability; with many, it settles close and stays close (the law of large numbers).
"70% chance of rain" doesn't promise rain; it says rain is more likely than not, so take an umbrella. That's why "3 heads in a row" says almost nothing about a coin, while 1,000 flips say a lot. Weather forecasts mix all three ways: past frequencies, computer models and expert judgement.

Events are sets of outcomes

  • The sample space Ω is the set of all outcomes: a die roll gives Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}
  • An event is a part of Ω: "even" = {2, 4, 6}
OperationSymbolDie (A = even, B = greater than 3)

Every probability question starts the same way: what is Ω, and which part of it is my event?
Think of Ω as a room with everyone in it; an event is a circle on the floor. Mutually exclusive events (even and odd) can't happen together: their circles don't overlap, A ∩ B = ∅. Search engines use the same operations: AND, OR, NOT.

Choices in steps multiply

  • Multiplication rule: a choices, then b, then c → a × b × c results
  • 3 shirts × 2 trousers × 2 pairs of shoes = 12 outfits
  • A 4-digit PIN: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000
  • 8 small letters: 26⁸ ≈ 209 billion; with capitals and digits (62 choices): ≈ 218 trillion
Each extra step multiplies the total by its number of choices.
Ordering a meal: 3 starters, 4 mains, 2 desserts gives 3 × 4 × 2 = 24 menus without listing them. A 6-digit PIN has 100 times more options than a 4-digit one: each extra position multiplies by 10.

n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 1 counts the ways to put n things in order: 3! = 6

If swapping two choices gives a different result, order matters. If not, count the ordered picks and divide by k!.
Handshakes: 10 people, each shakes hands once with every other: 10 × 9 = 90 ordered pairs, but A-B is the same handshake as B-A, so 90 ÷ 2! = 45. Check with 4 people: AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, CD = 6 = 4 × 3 ÷ 2.

If events overlap, subtract the overlap once

  • Equally likely outcomes: P(A) = favourable ÷ total
  • Addition rule: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
  • P(heart or face card) = 13/52 + 12/52 − 3/52 = 22/52 ≈ 0.42
  • If A and B can't happen together, the overlap is 0: just add
Adding two events counts their overlap twice, so subtract it once.
The three axioms in plain words: every probability is between 0 and 1; the whole sample space has probability 1; separate events add up. Everything else follows from these. Always ask first: can both happen at once?

"At least one" is easier as "1 − none"

  • Complement rule: P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A): P(not a six) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6
  • Roll a die 4 times: P(no six) = (5/6)⁴ = 625 ÷ 1,296 ≈ 0.482
  • P(at least one six) = 1 − 0.482 = 0.518, a little more than half
When a question says "at least one", compute 1 − P(none) first.
"At least one" means one, two, three or four sixes: long to count. "None" is one simple case. Reliability works the same way: the chance that at least one backup system works is 1 − P(all fail).
Part B

Updating with information

5Conditional probability6Bayes' theorem
How new information changes the odds.

Conditioning means zooming in

  • P(A | B) = the chance of A once we know B happened: only outcomes in B count now
  • P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B): die, A = even, B = greater than 3: (2/6) ÷ (3/6) = 2/3
  • 100 emails: 30 spam, 31 contain "free", 24 both → P(spam | free) = 24/31 ≈ 0.77, but P(free | spam) = 24/30 = 0.80
Knowing B changes which group you look at. And P(A | B) is not P(B | A).
The chance a random person wears a coat might be 30%; if you know it's snowing, it's much higher. The extra information doesn't change the people, it changes which group you're looking at. Swapping A and B is a common mistake; Bayes (lesson 6) goes from one to the other.

Multiply along the path; independent events don't care

  • Multiplication rule: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A)
  • A box of 3 red and 5 blue, two draws without putting back: P(red, then blue) = 3/8 × 5/7 = 15/56
  • Independent: knowing A doesn't change B, P(B | A) = P(B), so P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B): heads and an even die = ½ × ½ = ¼
A coin has no memory: after five heads, the next flip is still 50/50 (the gambler's fallacy).
Lesson 3's multiplication rule multiplies numbers of choices; this one multiplies probabilities. If the first ball is put back, the box is the same for the second draw, so P(B | A) = P(B) = 5/8: the draws become independent. The emails are not independent: knowing "free" raised the chance of spam from 0.30 to 0.77.

Add up every way the evidence can happen

  • Law of total probability: P(B) = Σᵢ P(Aᵢ) × P(B | Aᵢ), over slices Aᵢ that don't overlap and cover everything
  • A smoke alarm over 1,000 evenings: a fire on 2 (beeps every time) → 2 beeps; burnt toast on 100 (beeps 30% of the time) → 30 beeps; 898 quiet evenings → 0
  • P(beep) = 0.002 × 1 + 0.10 × 0.30 = 0.032
Split the world into separate situations, find the event's chance in each, and add.
Like the slices of a cake: no overlaps, nothing left out. A situation that never causes the event (a quiet evening) simply adds 0. To keep it simple, a fire and burnt toast never happen on the same evening.

Bayes: always ask for the base rate

  • P(A | B) = P(A) × P(B | A) ÷ P(B): from P(evidence | cause) to P(cause | evidence)
  • Smoke alarm: P(fire | beep) = 0.002 × 1 ÷ 0.032 ≈ 6% (2 of the 32 beeps)
  • Medical test on 1,000 people: 1% sick (10), test finds 90% (9), 9% false alarms on the 990 healthy (89) → P(sick | positive) = 9 ÷ 98 ≈ 9%
  • Raise "how common" to 10%: the same positive test means ≈ 53%
When the cause is rare, most positive results are false alarms, even with a good test.
Counts of people are the easiest way to use Bayes. The alarm is reliable, but fires are rare and burnt toast is common, so most beeps come from toast; a 6% chance of fire is still serious, so you always check. This is why a positive screening result is usually followed by a second, more accurate test.

Frequentist vs Bayesian: 7 heads in 10 tosses

  • Frequentist: "The true probability is a fixed but unknown number. Let's estimate it from the data." → 7 ÷ 10 = 70%
  • Bayesian: "I start with some uncertainty and combine that prior belief with the evidence." With no preference (a uniform prior, 50%): (heads + 1) ÷ (tosses + 2) = 8 ÷ 12 = 66.7%, updated after every toss
Frequentist: estimate the probability from the data. Bayesian: start from a prior and update it with every toss.
Same 10 tosses, H H T H H T H H T H. After one head the frequentist share is already 100%, while the Bayesian moves only to 66.7%: the prior keeps a few tosses from causing an extreme answer. The Bayesian estimate after each toss: 50%, 66.7%, 75%, 60%, 66.7%, 71.4%, 62.5%, 66.7%, 70%, 63.6%, 66.7%. The rule (heads + 1) ÷ (tosses + 2) is like starting with one imaginary head and one imaginary tail. In statistics books the uniform prior is Beta(1, 1) and the posterior after 7 heads and 3 tails is Beta(8, 4), with average 8 ÷ 12 = 0.667. The prior is centred on 50%, so it pulls the estimate a little toward 50%.

Both use the observed data; they differ in what a probability means. The frequentist estimate is the best guess from the data, not proof: with only 10 tosses it is quite uncertain. The Bayesian answer is a whole updated belief. The smoke alarm and the medical test on the previous slides worked the same way: the base rate was the prior. Research papers and A/B tests usually report frequentist confidence intervals; Bayesian updating is used when prior knowledge matters or data is scarce.
Part C

Random variables

7Random variables8Common distributions9More than one variable
Outcomes as numbers, the patterns they follow, and how two of them move together.

The expected value is the long-run average

  • A random variable X is a number that depends on chance; its distribution lists its values and their chances
  • Dice game: lose 1 on 1-3 (3/6), win 1 on 4-5 (2/6), win 3 on 6 (1/6)
  • E[X] = Σ value × probability = −1 × 3/6 + 1 × 2/6 + 3 × 1/6 ≈ 0.33 points per game
  • Spread: Var(X) ≈ 2.22, standard deviation σ ≈ 1.49 points
You never win exactly 0.33 points in one game; it's what the average settles to over many games.
A game is worth playing in the long run when its expected value is above 0; casino games are designed to be negative for the player. Over 1,000 games you'd expect to be about 333 points ahead, but half of all single games cost you a point. The PMF gives the chance of each value; the CDF, P(X ≤ x), the chance of that value or less (3/6, 5/6, 6/6 here). For continuous variables (waiting times) the chance of an exact value is 0: probability is the area under the density curve.

Recognise the pattern, pick the distribution

  • A distribution is a ready-made pattern for chance; its parameters set the details
  • Count yeses in n tries → Binomial (average n × p); events per hour → Poisson (average λ); waiting time → Exponential; clustered measurements → Normal (average μ, spread σ)
  • Also: Bernoulli (one yes/no), Geometric (tries until the first yes), Uniform (any value in a range)
Recognise the situation, pick the matching distribution, set its parameter: you get its probabilities, average and spread for free.
Like clothing sizes: you don't design a new shirt for every person; you pick the right pattern and adjust the size. Call centres plan staff with Poisson counts and Exponential waits; quality control uses the Binomial for faulty items.

The 68-95-99.7 rule

  • For a Normal distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ: about 68% of values fall within 1σ of the mean, 95% within 2σ, and 99.7% within 3σ
  • Exam scores averaging 70 with σ = 10: about 95% score between 50 and 90
For bell-shaped data, the mean and the standard deviation tell you where almost everything lies.
Heights, blood pressure, measurement errors and exam scores are often summarised with a mean and a standard deviation for this reason. The next slide explains why bells are everywhere.

Add up many small effects and you get a bell

  • One coin toss is just 0 or 1. Toss 100 coins, count the heads, and repeat: the counts pile up in a bell around 50
  • Why the middle wins: far more sequences land near the middle. 4 tosses: ways to get 0-4 heads = 1, 4, 6, 4, 1
  • Central Limit Theorem: the sum or average of many independent values tends to be Normal, whatever each single value looks like
Heights, errors and exam scores are bell-shaped because each is a sum of many small, independent effects.
Two cautions: it's an approximation that improves as more values are added (2 tosses are far from a bell, 100 are very close), and it needs more values when each one is lopsided; a rule of thumb is at least about 10 heads and 10 tails. "Tossing a coin gives a Normal" is true only for the total over many tosses, never for a single toss.

Two rules hold everything together

  • Sum rule: P(sunny) = 0.45 + 0.15 = 0.60
  • Product rule: P(sunny, high) = P(sunny) × P(high | sunny) = 0.60 × 0.75 = 0.45
  • Not independent: 0.60 × 0.55 = 0.33 ≠ 0.45
The sum rule and the product rule: conditional probability, total probability and Bayes all follow from these two.
The totals at the edges are "marginal" probabilities, which is where "marginalization" comes from. Bayes from the two rules: P(sunny | high) = 0.75 × 0.60 ÷ 0.55 ≈ 0.82: on a high-sales day, it was probably sunny. These two rules are also the starting point of many AI methods.

Correlation is not causation

  • Covariance gives the direction: do two numbers tend to be above their averages at the same time?
  • Correlation r gives direction and strength without units: −1 (perfect opposite) through 0 to +1 (perfect together)
  • Ice cream sales and drownings have a strong positive correlation, but hot weather drives both: a confounder
Correlation tells you that two things move together, not why.
Two dancers: one steps forward whenever the other does (positive), one steps back (negative), or they ignore each other (none). Covariance's size depends on the units; correlation divides by both standard deviations so it stays between −1 and +1. If two variables are independent, their correlation is 0. Investors combine assets with low correlation to reduce risk.

Key takeaways

  1. Probability measures how likely, from 0 to 1; many trials reveal it.
  2. Start every problem with Ω and the event as a set.
  3. Choices multiply; divide by k! when order doesn't matter.
  4. Overlaps: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).
  5. "At least one" = 1 − P(none).
  6. Conditioning zooms in, and P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A).
  7. Bayes: always ask for the base rate; a Bayesian updates a prior with every new piece of evidence.
  8. The expected value is the long-run average.
  9. Sums of many small effects make a bell (CLT).
  10. Correlation is not causation.
